## Why PCBs? Aren't breadboards easier?

Well, maybe. Ben Eater's decision to use breadboards was pedagogical in nature. Circuits built on a breadboard are easier to modify and debug and easier to both explain and understand, since all of the components and connections are in plain sight and changes can be made quickly.

But... the benefits mostly end there. Technical limitations like parasitic capacitance aside, I found breadboards immensely difficult to work with when scaling up Eater's original design. I simply did not have the patience to carefully bend and route each and every wire to look pretty. See Figure 1 for my wire spaghetti with only the clock, program counter, and ALU finished.

### Figure 1. Breadboards get messy if you're not careful

![A mess of wires](/assets/images/wire-spaghetti.jpg){: width="75%"}

It was at this point that I decided on a different approach. Integrating all the wiring onto a printed circuit board (PCB) would help with my wire spaghetti problem and also offer a more compact, permanent design that I could later assemble into some sort of display. Furthermore, it would look badass.

## PCB Design for Digital Circuits: A Novice's Guide for Novices

Herein are some tips and tricks I've learned throughout the process that might help anyone else hoping to build a PCB version of an Eater-style CPU or other digital logic circuit. Everything I know I have learned from this project, as I began with zero experience with board design or assembly.

### PCB Design and Ordering

Click **Switch to PCB Editor** in the menu panel of the schematic editor. This will open the PCB editor window. Then, click **File → Board Setup** to open the board setup dialog. Click **Import Settings from Another Board** and choose one of the PCB files from [my Github](https://github.com/scradam1/breadboard-cpu). Click **Select All** and confirm. This will import all of the fabrication parameters that jibe with JLCPCB's capabilities.

Next, click **Update PCB from Schematic** and confirm in the dialog. This will populate your PCB with all of the components from your schematic.

#### Cost

- **Keep board size under 100 mm x 100 mm**. JLCPCB imposes at least a $20 surcharge for larger boards but does not state this anywhere on their website.

### PCB Assembly

#### Tools

Many guides on the Internet suggest that beginners compile a vast amount of equipment and materials prior to doing any PCB work. This can be quite an investment. Here are the **bare minimum** items you need to assemble PCBs at home:

- **Soldering station with temperature control**: I bought a [cheap but highly reviewed station from Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CDNWLJWJ). The temperature control lets you solder at a temperature just high enough to melt the solder, but no higher, limiting corrosion on the tip of the iron. You could probably get away with an even cheaper one without temperature control. [This Reddit thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/soldering/comments/1n1f2hi/soldering_station_buying_mega_guide/) describes some of the things to look out for when buying.
- **Leaded solder**: Since it was my first time soldering, I went with the "easy way out" and used leaded solder, which melts at a lower temperature than its lead-free counterpart and is thus considered a little easier to manipulate.
- **"Helping hand" clips**: I bought [the most bargain-basement, crappy helping hand](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000P42O3C) I could find. It works fine. Just something to hold up the board while you have soldering wire in one hand and a soldering iron in the other.
- **Wire cutters**: You'll need these to clip off the leads after soldering your through-hole components.

And that's it! There are *many* more things that can make your life easier: extra flux paste, a magnifying glass, tweezers, desoldering wick, a hot air gun, *etc.* But the bare minimum cost me about **$70 total**. Doesn't have to break the bank!

#### Safety Considerations

Soldering is pretty benign but still carries its risks. Here are a few things to keep in mind:

- **Soldering irons are <em>hot</em>!** I use mine at 550 °F (290 °C). Always be aware of where your iron is and what's around it. Don't manipulate other things while the iron is in your hand; it's very easy to lose track of where the tip is. So far, I only have one tiny burn.
- **Leaded vs. lead-free solder**: If you have children, pets, or anyone else in your household who might be interested in licking your circuit boards, I would suggest using lead-free solder. I do not and so am unconcerned with the toxicity of lead (though I still wash my hands after touching it!!)
- **Flux**: The rosin flux used in the core of most soldering wire (leaded *and* lead-free) is what really warrants more attention health-wise. Applying heat to the soldering wire burns off and vaporizes some of the flux, creating the smoke often associated with soldering. This smoke is unpleasant (though not terrible). Try not to breathe it in, and consider opening a window if soldering inside.
- **Your workspace**: I did this project in an apartment and had to do my soldering on the kitchen table. Not optimal, but just take precautions. I lay down plastic sheeting on the table and place a piece of cardboard on top of the sheeting. Then, I do my soldering on top. This prevents bits of metal and splatters of flux from contaminating any surfaces you care about.
